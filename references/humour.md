# Humor — how Lussaro is funny

**American English.** The file keeps its original name; the spelling inside it doesn't.

---

## The calibration

The brand register is polished and aspirational. That rules out most comedy — no jokes, no bits, no punchlines, nothing that interrupts elegant copy to be funny.

What survives is **the dry edge**: one unglamorous true observation placed inside otherwise composed writing. Not a joke. A raised eyebrow.

**The anchor, and it's Robert's own line:**

> "Makes you feel like that car is yours, and not a clapped out Lamborghini you got a good deal on."

Look at what that does. The first half is the aspirational promise. The second half is a knife, and it's funny — but it's also doing the sales work, because it names exactly what the customer is afraid of getting. **That's the model. The humor and the argument are the same sentence.**

---

## The rule that comes out of it

**The edge shows up when we're making a point, never when we're being funny.**

Robert isn't funny when he's chatting. He's funny when he's explaining why the cheap quote is a bad deal. Humor here is a byproduct of candor, not a decoration on top of it — so if a line is funny but isn't arguing for something, cut it.

**Once per piece.** Twice in something long. The scarcity is what makes it read as confidence rather than as trying.

---

## Mechanics

**Deadpan, always.** State it flat. The sentence around it stays completely serious.

**Never explain it.** No "(kidding)," no wink, no softener afterward.

**No puns.** Not once, not ironically, not in a heading.

**No exclamation marks. No emoji.** Both are fatal to the register and to the joke.

**No profanity in site copy.** Available for social only.

**Put the sharp word last.** "Clapped out Lamborghini you got a good deal on" ends on the phrase that stings. Almost every line that works does this.

**Then move on.** One dry line, then straight back to something useful. Never two in a row.

## Where it aims

### At the industry — the primary target

This is where Robert is genuinely sharp, because he has a real argument underneath. His position: cheap operators push volume, volume wears cars out, and you end up paying less for something worse.

> "A lot of companies undercut the market here. Often the cars aren't as well kept, because of the sheer volume they push."
> "Contact us for pricing is a price. It's just not one anybody wants written down."
> "Every fleet in this city photographs well at 60 miles an hour."

**Never name a company.** Robert does this instinctively — *"a lot of companies"* — and it's both classier and safer. Aim at the practice.

**And only when we genuinely do better.** The volume line works because our cars are kept sharp. A line about hidden fees would be hypocrisy if our own fees weren't published.

### At Miami — affectionate, from a chosen insider

Robert is Canadian and moved here on purpose. That's the right angle: someone who chose this city and finds its theater funny, rather than someone born into it or sneering at it from outside.

> "Ocean Drive at eight in the evening moves at eleven miles an hour. There's time to be admired."
> "Nothing in this city is more than twenty minutes away, and everything takes an hour."
> "A Lamborghini in Miami is roughly a Honda anywhere else."

### At ourselves — sparingly, and only about obsession

Two limits, and they matter more in an aspirational register than they would in a casual one.

**1. Joke about obsession, never about means.** The difference decides whether a self-deprecating line builds the brand or quietly damages it:

> **Wrong:** "I own six exotic cars and a folding chair."
> **Right:** "I bought the first one with my savings, which felt like a business decision at the time."

The first implies undercapitalized — and the reader is about to send a $1,000 deposit and trust a stranger with a supercar. The second says the money went into the product.

**2. Never in the opening line.** Modesty works once the reader trusts you. An opening joke at our own expense frames everything after it.

**And never about the service.** Robert can be the punchline. The car arriving clean and on time never is.

### At the clichés — gently, never at the reader

The person renting a Huracán for photographs is a real customer and a welcome one.

> "The Cullinan photographs beautifully parked. That isn't a criticism."
> "Most Huracán rentals cover about forty miles. The other sixty are included anyway."

**The test:** if the line makes the reader feel judged for wanting the car, rewrite it so they're in on the joke rather than the subject of it.

---

## Off limits

- **The service.** Delivery times, cleanliness, whether the car shows up. Never a punchline.
- **Speed, or anything that reads as encouragement to drive badly.** This is a liability issue, not a taste one — see the Urus story in `stories.md` for exactly why.
- **Money problems**, the reader's or anyone's.
- **Real customers**, even anonymized. The five Google reviewers did us a favor.
- **Anyone's appearance, income, neighborhood, or accent.**

---

## Calibration

> **Too much:** "We both know you're renting this to post one story and expense it. No judgment. Actually, some judgment."
> — two jokes stacked, then it explains itself, then it insults the buyer. Also completely wrong for the register.

> **Right:** "Makes you feel like that car is yours, and not a clapped out Lamborghini you got a good deal on."
> — aspirational promise, sharp finish, and it argues for the price at the same time.

> **Too little:** "Our meticulously maintained vehicles ensure every client enjoys a truly exceptional experience."
> — polished with nothing underneath. This is the failure mode of the elevated register, and it's a bigger risk here than being too funny.
