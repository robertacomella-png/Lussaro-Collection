# Stories — the recurring anecdotes

Two real ones, both from Robert, August 2026. Everything here happened.

**One story per piece, maximum.** A blog post gets one. A landing page usually gets none. Reuse across pieces is fine and good — a story told three times over a year becomes part of the brand. It just can't appear twice in the same piece.

---

# 1. The first Urus rental

**The strongest thing the brand has. Handle carefully.**

## What happened

Robert's first Maybach booking had gone fine, which is what makes the rest of it land — he had no reason to expect trouble.

The Urus went out for the first time on a three-day booking, arranged through a broker, to a young athlete. Airport drop-off, rules explained, everything normal.

A few hours into the first night the tracker showed the car had already touched 100 mph. Robert sent a warning and went to sleep.

By morning the tracker had recorded **over 100 mph for roughly twenty minutes straight, topping out at 137**, on a run home from a party. Robert killed the car remotely. It was parked at an Airbnb an hour away.

He drove out with the spare key. When he opened the car it smelled of alcohol — some had been spilled inside. He knocked and banged on the windows for **an hour** before anyone came to the door.

The renter refused to hand back the keys, arguing that the contract never said he couldn't exceed 100 mph. He put his lawyer on the phone. Then his father. Robert held the line: the contract prohibits speed racing.

He called the police. They arrived, and with them standing there the father was still on speakerphone demanding to know exactly where the contract said 100 mph.

**And this is the moment the story exists for.** Robert had every incentive to lay it all out — the alcohol, the 137, all of it, in front of police. Instead he said only that what the renter had done would one hundred percent constitute speed racing, and declined to go into further detail.

They handed back the keys. The police left. Robert took the car.

The renter had it for three days and drove it for a day and a half. He lost the balance of the booking, because a supercar can't be rebooked at that notice, and he lost his security deposit.

## Why it's usable

It is not a war story. **It's the origin of every policy the business has.** The tracker, the insurance requirement, the screening, the deposit, "no speed racing" in the contract, the refusal to rent to underqualified drivers — all of it traces to one twenty-minute stretch of highway on the first time the Urus went out.

That's what earns it a place in copy: it explains *why the rules exist*, from a business that was learning them in real time.

The restraint is the point, not the win. Robert got his car back and still left the kid a way to not be destroyed in front of police officers. Lead with that, not with the 137.

## Non-negotiable rules for telling it

- **"College basketball player" never appears.** Combined with a date and a city that's identifying. It becomes "a young renter," or just "a renter."
- **The alcohol stays vague.** Stating that an identifiable person likely drove drunk is a defamation exposure. "The car came back in a state" carries the meaning safely.
- **The 137 is a consequence, never a spectacle.** The story is about enforcement. It must never read as a fun fact about how fast the Urus goes — see the speed rule in `humour.md`.
- **No names.** Not the renter, not the broker, not the Airbnb, not the police department.
- **No humor anywhere in it.** This is the one story that gets told completely straight.

Anonymized it loses nothing. The lawyer, the father on speakerphone, the hour of knocking, the restraint — none of it needs a name.

# 2. How Lussaro started

## What happened

Robert was working in social media and marketing, living in Toronto. He kept traveling to Miami for fun and made friends who stuck. In his words, **"the lifestyle down here is intoxicating"** — he decided he wanted to live there.

He had always loved cars and wanted to build a business around them. The business became the route: it qualified him for the visa that let him move to the United States.

He describes the decision as one he **"made without thinking and just jumped into."**

The first car was a **Mercedes-Maybach GLS 600**, bought with his savings. Not the obvious choice for someone who loves cars — the obvious first buy is a Lamborghini. He chose it because it was, in his words, **"an affordable exotic, so to speak,"** with a high cap rate: cheap to acquire relative to what it rents for.

Six cars inside the first year. That Maybach is still in the fleet at $895 a day.

## Why it's usable

Most people in this industry start with money and buy a toy. Robert started with a city he wanted to live in, and the business was the way to get there. The stakes were never just financial.

It also establishes the temperament that runs through everything else: **he bought his first car on yield, not passion.** That's why he can recommend the S580 over the Maybach without ego, and why the pricing behaves the way it does. Operator first, enthusiast second.

And the anchor is checkable — the first car he ever bought is on the site right now.

## Rules for telling it

- **The visa stays out of copy** unless Robert says otherwise. He was asked twice and didn't take it up. Immigration status is easy to publish and impossible to unpublish. It stays here as background that explains the motivation.
- **Never turn it into a rags-to-riches arc.** He had savings and a career. The story is commitment, not hardship.
- **"The lifestyle down here is intoxicating"** is his phrase and can be used nearly as-is.
- **No tenure claims attached to it.** Telling the origin story doesn't license "years of experience." See `stats.md`.
