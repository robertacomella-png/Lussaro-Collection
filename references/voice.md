# Voice — Lussaro Collection

Read this before writing any customer-facing copy: landing pages, car pages, blog posts, meta descriptions, emails.

**American English throughout.** Robert is Canadian and writes "humour" and "colour" by habit; the brand does not.

---

## Who the brand is

Lussaro Collection is Robert's company. Marketing and social background, Toronto, kept visiting Miami until he decided to live there, and built the business that made it possible. Bought the first car — a Mercedes-Maybach GLS 600 — with his own savings, chosen on yield rather than passion. Six cars inside the first year.

That background matters because it explains the temperament: **operator first, enthusiast second.** He can recommend the cheaper car without ego because he's thinking about the fleet, not about which car is coolest. Most people in this industry bought a toy and rented it out to justify it. That is not this business.

## Person: "we," with room for "I"

**Default to "we."** It's how Robert already writes to customers — *"we got your deposit,"* *"we typically don't do this."*

**"I" is available where the line is genuinely personal** — an opinion, a promise about how the business is run, a story only he can tell. Self-deprecating lines only work in first person. Don't force "we" onto a sentence that's obviously one man talking.

Never invent staff. There is no team to hide behind, and the reviews name Robert personally.

---

## Register: polished, aspirational, with an edge

The brand sounds **premium and composed**. Elevated language is welcome. This is the register that sells a $2,195 Ferrari, and it is a deliberate choice — it is *not* a transcription of how Robert texts, which is much plainer.

But polished on its own is indistinguishable from every competitor in Miami. The thing that makes it Lussaro is the **edge underneath**: a willingness to say the unglamorous true thing in the middle of elegant copy.

Robert's own line is the model:

> "Makes you feel like that car is yours, and not a clapped out Lamborghini you got a good deal on."

Aspirational promise, then a knife. That sentence is the whole voice in one move.

### By surface

| Surface | Register |
|---|---|
| **Car pages** | Most elevated. Someone is deciding on a $2,195 booking. Elegant, confident, specific. The edge appears once at most. |
| **Landing pages** | Elevated, but doing real work. One sharp observation per page, the rest genuinely useful. |
| **Blog / long-form** | The most room. Full opinions, the dry lines, the stories. |
| **FAQ, terms, pricing** | Voice off. Numbers and policy, stated plainly. Nobody wants elegance next to a deposit figure. |

**No profanity in site copy.** Available for social only.

## Sentence mechanics

Robert's own writing is fast and stripped down. The brand voice is more polished, but it inherits the discipline:

- **No throat-clearing.** Lead with the answer, then the context. He quotes a price in four words; copy shouldn't take a paragraph.
- **Short sentences carry the point, longer ones carry the explanation.** Vary hard.
- Contractions always.
- **Cut any sentence that only introduces the next one.**
- One idea per paragraph. Two to four sentences.
- Subheads that say something, not labels.
- Bold sparingly, for the one number that matters.

### The question ending

Every message Robert sends ends in a question. *What are you looking to rent? Does that work for you? What are you thinking? Do you wanna lock it in?* He closes by asking, never by telling, and hands the decision back every time.

**This should shape CTAs.** The site currently uses imperatives — "Book on WhatsApp," "Call Now," "Reserve your Miami exotic." None of them sound like the business. *"What are you thinking?"* does. Where a CTA can be a question without going limp, make it one.

## Vocabulary

**Words the business actually uses.** Prefer these over synonyms:

> lock it in · put you in [a car] · booked out · rented out · to be honest · what are you thinking · clapped out · well kept · our [wheel] guy

**Avoid:** the corporate register Robert reaches for when he's trying to sound professional — *"we're priced competitively," "unfortunately."* It's the one place his writing goes generic, and it's what every competitor sounds like.

There is **no banned-word list.** An earlier version of this file banned "elevate," "unlock," "experience" and similar. Robert uses "experience" naturally and likes the elevated register. Judge lines on whether they're *true and specific*, not on vocabulary.

## The moves that define the brand

These are documented behaviors, observed in real customer conversations. Not aspirations.

**1. Recommend the cheaper car.** Under price pressure Robert's reflex is to ask the budget and offer the S580 at $445 — *"its just as nice as the maybach."* He undercuts his own premium car to keep someone comfortable. Copy should do the same: say plainly when the cheaper car is the better call.

**2. Fix it before you mention it.** Twice observed. Car running late → the previous renter had already been charged and the credit applied. Curbed wheel → the wheel guy was already booked. The business does not message to discuss a problem; it messages to report one already being solved.

**3. Apologize only when it's ours.** "Sorry" opens every message where Lussaro is at fault. It appears nowhere in the message about the damaged wheel. Precision, not coldness — and most operators get it backwards.

**4. Evidence, unprompted.** Timestamped photos attached before anyone asks for them.

**5. Never close the door.** Every refusal carries a way back in. *"The car is booked out already but I can put you in any other car you like."* No is always followed by an alternative.

**6. Explain what we can't change; flex on what we can.** Robert moves on his own margin without much resistance and holds firm on pass-through cost — then *explains* it. The $499 insurance line got a real justification: full coverage on a Lamborghini for an uninsured driver costs what it costs.

**7. Real numbers, never rounded.** He quotes $445 and $995 instantly, unhedged. See `stats.md` — those are the only numbers that go in copy.

## Hard rules

- **Never claim or imply tenure.** The company was formed December 2025 and started trading January 2026. No "years of experience," no "long established," no "trusted for years." Registration is public. Say nothing about age; nobody notices the absence.
- **Never claim fleet size or ranking superiority.** Not "largest fleet," not "#1 in Miami," not "best in Florida." Six cars is six cars, and one competitor lists 80+. What's true and stronger: *every review we have is five stars.*
- **Never invent a customer, a review, or a booking.** Real reviews live in `src/data/reviews.js`, quoted verbatim or not at all.
- **Never publish utilization, revenue, or booking volume.** Internal only.
- **Never state a number that isn't in `stats.md`.**
- **Don't name competitors.** Aim at the practice. Robert does this instinctively — *"a lot of companies"* — and it's the right instinct.
- **Never assert a fact the research can't confirm.** Seat counts, trim details, options.

## Tells that it's AI-written

Reread and cut anything matching:

- **The tricolon.** "Performance, prestige, and concierge-level service." Three abstract nouns in a row is the loudest tell.
- **"Not just X, but Y."**
- **Symmetrical paragraphs** — every one the same length, every one three sentences.
- **A closing paragraph that restates the opening.**
- **Hedged enthusiasm.** "Whether you're celebrating a milestone or simply treating yourself…"
- **Adjective stacking.** "Premium, high-performance luxury vehicles."
- **Elevated language with nothing underneath it.** This is the real risk in the aspirational register: elegance is easy to fake, specifics aren't. Every polished paragraph needs a real number, a real observation, or a real position inside it.
- **Copy that would work for any company in any city.** Swap in a competitor's name — if it still reads fine, cut it.
- **No opinion anywhere.** The most reliable tell of all.
